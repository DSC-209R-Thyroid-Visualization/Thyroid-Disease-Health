Chasing the Butterfly

One patient's path through Graves' treatment, draw by draw

Methimazole Methimazole is an antithyroid intervention I have been on for over two years. Other possible interventions include radioactive iodine, or a thyroidectomy for surgical removal.
Why "the butterfly"? The thyroid has two lobes, one on each side of the windpipe, joined by a thin bridge called the isthmus. Spread across the front of the neck, they look like a butterfly's wings. It weighs only about 20 grams, yet its hormones set the pace of nearly every cell in the body. Too high, or too low? Graves' disease makes the thyroid overactive (hyper); Hashimoto's makes it underactive (hypo). Staying out of euthyroid range for long takes a toll: too high strains the heart and bones, too low brings fatigue, weight gain and high cholesterol. Treatment is a balancing act, and too much methimazole can tip Graves' into hypo.

Project Write-up

Over the past nine years, health complications led to my diagnosis with Graves' disease, an autoimmune disease that attacks the thyroid. For the last two and a half years my endocrinologist has managed my thyroid with methimazole. This visualization lines up my treatment, lab results and journal entries over time, and compares each blood draw with the 50 patients in a Kaggle thyroid dataset whose TSH and T3 were closest to mine.

The idea came from UCSD Professor Emeritus Dr. Larry Smarr's "Future Patient" project, where he visualized his own biomarkers over time. I found it inspiring and wanted something similar for myself. Patient portals like MyChart show lab results and basic charts, but make it hard to see how biomarkers relate to each other, to treatment, or to how I actually felt. The body is one connected system, and methimazole is known to affect the liver in some patients, so it's worth comparing my thyroid with my liver and other labs on one timeline.

Design rationale

The title refers to the thyroid's butterfly shape across the front of the neck, and the subtitle says the page follows my treatment one blood draw at a time. The main question is "Could I tell how my thyroid was doing just by how I felt?" Each of my 17 blood draws has a card that puts my journal entry from that day next to what my labs said, and the two often disagree. In December 2024 I wrote "I feel back to normal" while my labs were still slightly overactive.

Showing this statically would mean lining up dates, finding the journal entry for each draw, and looking up the Kaggle comparison by hand, and comparing my thyroid with dozens of other labs would be close to impossible. Interaction brings it together and turns the page into something I can actually use at a doctor's visit. The play button under the title tells my story up to diagnosis, and hovering over the pill bottle and thyroid icons explains methimazole and the thyroid. In the main chart, clicking a dot or the status strip, or using Previous and Next, opens that draw's card: date, dose, journal entry, lab results against their normal ranges, status, and the Kaggle comparison. A marker drops across the dose, TSH, Free T4, T3 and status rows so the same moment is easy to find in each one.

The timeline shows dose as a step line, since a dose holds until it changes, and my labs as lines with markers, which are familiar and easy to read. TSH is on a log scale because it moves across orders of magnitude. The mint green band is the UCSD normal range for each lab. We kept color for thyroid status only, on a warm-to-cool scale: red for overactive, since the body runs hot, blue for underactive, since it runs cold, lighter shades for "slightly," and green for normal. Lines and dose are grey, so an out-of-range result shows up in the color of the status it points to. Hollow dots are results below the detection limit.

The "Beyond the thyroid" section covers all 73 of my lab tests since 2017. It opens on four labs that changed while my thyroid was overactive, next to my thyroid levels, and the menu shows any other group or all of them. Hovering shows the nearest result on every chart at once, and clicking drops a marker that isn't tied to a blood draw. Since these span nine years, dragging across a chart zooms in, the zoom stays when you switch groups, and Reset returns to the full view. With this many charts the lines share one grey, and the green normal band is a bit stronger since the charts are small. Out-of-range results on non-thyroid labs are a larger black dot, because high or low there says nothing about the thyroid.

We tried several alternatives. Buttons switching between raw, normalized and overlay views were dropped for a single page, since each view was really a static chart behind a button. An early linked timeline of dose and labs grew into the timeline next to the cards. A TSH ridgeline against Kaggle groups was less intuitive than the cards, and a treatment comparison of pills, radioactive iodine and surgery worked fine as a static chart. A separate view of draws where my status disagreed with the Kaggle patients is now part of each card instead of its own interaction. Parallel sets on tumor and goitre status were hard to fit into our story. Zoom on the main chart was distracting with only 17 draws, so we kept it only in the lower charts, which go back to 2017.

For the data, each draw's status uses the UCSD reference ranges (TSH 0.27–4.2 mIU/L, Free T4 0.93–1.7 ng/dL, Total T3 0.8–2.0 ng/mL). Overactive covers hyperthyroid and subclinical hyper, and underactive covers hypothyroid and subclinical hypo. Kaggle diagnoses were grouped by the first letter of the target code (A–D hyperthyroid, E–H hypothyroid, "-" normal), and other codes and ages over 100 were dropped. The closest 50 Kaggle patients are found by Euclidean distance on log TSH and T3, each scaled by its standard deviation, with my T3 converted to nmol/L to match Kaggle. Using 20 or 100 patients gives a similar pattern.

The design does hide a few things. A Kaggle label is one diagnosis per patient, made with T4, symptoms and history, and those patients were referred for thyroid testing rather than drawn from the general public. My journal entries are also inconsistent: some were written before a blood draw and some after I saw the results.

Development process

We each came up with different ideas, and the group liked the angle of a personal health journey. Each of us built prototypes in Jupyter notebooks in our shared GitHub repo, then we voted to combine three ideas: my thyroid levels over time, the 50 closest Kaggle patients to see how my diagnosis compares to a larger group, and my wider biomarker history to see how the thyroid may or may not affect other labs. We all contributed to the data transformations and to ideas for the tool.

In 2017 I began experiencing heart palpitations on a regular basis. My doctor at the time had me checked out for heart-related issues, which all came back negative…

Believing that I was just experiencing anxiety, I continued with heart palpitations for another 7 years…

After I began losing weight, having hand tremors, experiencing an increase in palpitations, and hair loss, I decided to see a new primary care physician, who decided to test me for thyroid-related conditions, given that my mom has Hashimoto's disease…

I came back as positive for hyperthyroidism, and my antibodies returned positive for Graves' disease.

Now, under specialized care, I have been on the road to recovery and to better understand my body and how interventions are affecting me.

This is my data and this is my journey.
