Chasing the Butterfly

One patient's path through Graves' treatment, draw by draw

Methimazole Methimazole is an antithyroid intervention I have been on for over two years. Other possible interventions include radioactive iodine, or a thyroidectomy for surgical removal.
Why "the butterfly"? The thyroid has two lobes, one on each side of the windpipe, joined by a thin bridge called the isthmus. Spread across the front of the neck, they look like a butterfly's wings. It weighs only about 20 grams, yet its hormones set the pace of nearly every cell in the body. Too high, or too low? Graves' disease makes the thyroid overactive (hyper); Hashimoto's makes it underactive (hypo). Staying out of euthyroid range for long takes a toll: too high strains the heart and bones, too low brings fatigue, weight gain and high cholesterol. Treatment is a balancing act, and too much methimazole can tip Graves' into hypo.

Project Write-up

Over the past 9 years I have undergone health complications that ultimately resulted in being diagnosed with Graves' disease, an autoimmune disease that attacks my thyroid. Over the past two and a half years I have undergone treatment with an endocrinologist who has been moderating my thyroid levels through methimazole. The visualization primarily focuses on my personal data, but includes diagnoses from the 50 patients in a Kaggle thyroid health dataset who were the closest to my TSH and T3 levels to compare my diagnosis with a community of other patients. The ability to visualize my data over time, aligned with my treatment allows me to contextualize events with my biomarkers and journal entries. This creates a meaningful tool as I continue through treatment.

The motivation for this project comes from UCSD Professor Emeritus Dr. Larry Smarr's "Future Patient" project, which used Dr. Smarr's time series biomarkers to create a visualization of his own health journey. I found this project fascinating and inspiring, and I wanted to create something similar for myself, using my own data to track my progress. Similar to Future Patient, the visualization is broken into multiple parts, with a high level of interactivity that assists the user in understanding various events.

Patient portals such as MyChart provide access to laboratory results and basic time series charts, but they offer limited functionality for understanding relationships between different biomarkers over long periods of time. Considering the body is an interconnected system, I am interested in understanding how my thyroid and treatment may or may not influence other biomarker groups. For instance, methimazole is known to affect liver functions in some patients, making it valuable to compare my thyroid biomarkers with liver results on a shared timeline. As the biomarker groups grow, interactivity is the most practical way to navigate the data.

The title was chosen to be light and playful, referencing the thyroid as the 'Butterfly' organ, which it is often referred to as it is shaped like a butterfly across the front of the neck. The subtitle was chosen as a way to convey that the visualizations are a time series through the treatment process. The title question "Could I tell how my thyroid was doing just by how I felt?" allows the user to understand that as they explore the visualization, they will be presented with information that connects to personal to a personal experience.

Presenting this information statically would be challenging. The user would need to time align the medical information and manually pull up a journal entry for each blood draw, and then compare the results of the blood draw with the kaggle data. Furthermore, its would be extremly difficult to compare my thyroid biomarkers to my other biomarkers within their classified groups. Interactivity allows for personal experience to connect with medical data while also providing some tools that extend the project beyond visual storytelling and into a practical tool that can be used in patient visits.

The visualization contains several different interactive features including hover, click, drag to zoom, animation playback, flashcards, and guided callouts. Pressing play under the title tells my story leading up to diagnosis, and hovering over the pill bottle and thyroid icons gives background on methimazole and on the thyroid itself. In the main chart, clicking on a data point or pressing 'next' on the flashcard, shows that draw's date, treatment dose, lab results, status, journal entry, and a comparison to the kaggle dataset. Clicking a data point also drops a marker across the linked graphs, allowing that reference point to be easily identified across the graphs. Beneath the labs, the My Status strip shows what stage of thyroid disease I was in a particular pint in time. The explination of the color codings match inside the flashcard under the 'What my labs said' section. Scrolling down to the 'Beyond the thyroid' section allows the user to compare my thyroid data with all the other biomarkers that I have taken since 2017. Here the user can select a grouping of biomarkers from a drop down list. Comparisons can be made between the chosen group and the thyroid levels. The functionality is expanded in these charts. There is hover over that spans all graphs and when a marker is dropped, its not locked to a specific point. Since the time series is now expanded over 9 years, the user can click and drag across a graph, to zoom in. The zoom level will persist as different groups of biomarkers are selected. The reset button can be used to reset the zoom level.

Methimazole dose is represented by a step line, which shows the dose I was taking for a specified period of time. Blue was chosen as its color encoding because methimazole is intended to push the thyroid toward an underactive state. The line chart with markers was chosen for the blood draw time series data because it is easy to interpret and is universally understood. The background of each chart has a mint green section, which represents the "normal" range for the biomarker according to UCSD Health. The dot and line colors were chosen to look appealing, while being distinguishable from one another. The 'My Status' uses a diverging color pallette, with green as the midpoint. As the stages become closer to hyper thyroid, the colors diverge towards red, and as the stages become closer to hypo thyroid, the colors diverge towards blue. In the "Beyond the Thyroid" section the colors are unified across the line plots since there are so many that can be displayed. The green normal range color is further amplified since the charts are smaller and can be more difficult to read.

Alternatives were explored that used buttons to switch between multiple views. However, we decided that a single page design would be more effective for user experience. An early design also used a linked timeline view combining medication dosage and laboratory results, which eventually evolved into the timeline displayed alongside the flashcards. We experimented with a TSH ridgeline visualization to compare my results with Kaggle patient groups, but it was less intuitive than using the flashcards. We also considered a treatment comparison view showing outcomes for medication, radioactive iodine, and surgery; however, because the comparison could be communicated effectively with a simple static chart it was removed. The main biomarker charts originally included zoom functionality, but this feature became distracting and was removed. Zooming was retained in the smaller laboratory charts because they span a much longer time period, with some non-thyroid related results dating back to 2017. Another discarded feature allowed users to examine cases where my diagnosis differed from similar Kaggle patients. This information is now integrated directly into each flashcard rather than requiring a separate interaction. We also explored an interactive visualization to understand tumor and goitre status, but it was difficult to integrate into our final concept.

Various data transformations were completed for this assignment. The status of each draw is binned to the UCSD reference range (TSH 0.27–4.2 mIU/L, Free T4 0.93–1.7 ng/dL,Total T3 0.8–2.0 ng/mL). Overactive covers hyperthyroid and subclinical hyper; underactive covers hypothyroid and subclinical hypo. The kaggle diagnosis groups use a target range where A-D represents a person in a hyperthyroid stat, and E-H represents a patient in a hypothyroid stats. In the kaggle dataset a person in a normal state is represented by a '-'. Some data cleaning was complete to drop unrelated codes and ages over 100. To determine the Lookalikes the Euclidean distance on [log10 TSH, T3], each scaled by its standard deviation and limited to the nearest 50 patients. Kaggle measures T3 in nmol/L, therefore my T3 was converted to identify lookalikes.

The design obscures a couple items that need addressing. A Kaggle label is one diagnosis per patient, made with T4, symptoms, and history. Furthermore, Kaggle patients were referred for thyroid testing, not the generaly public. Another important obscurity is taht my journal entries are inconsistent. Sometimes I would enter my journal entry before my blood testing, and sometimes after I received the results.

As a group we each formulated different ideas and the group liked the personal angle of a personal health journey visualization. We each generated different prototypes, primarily by sharing jupyter notebooks over a shared project repo in GitHub. The outcome ended up being a vote to combine several ideas. The first being a view of my most pulled thyroid levels, the second being to integrate the 50 closest Kaggle dataset patients to understand how my diagnosis compares to a larger community, the third was to expand my biomarker set to view how thyroid may or may not interact with other biomarker categories. We all contributed to various data transformations and various tool ideas.

In 2017 I began experiencing heart palpitations on a regular basis. My doctor at the time had me checked out for heart-related issues, which all came back negative…

Believing that I was just experiencing anxiety, I continued with heart palpitations for another 7 years…

After I began losing weight, having hand tremors, experiencing an increase in palpitations, and hair loss, I decided to see a new primary care physician, who decided to test me for thyroid-related conditions, given that my mom has Hashimoto's disease…

I came back as positive for hyperthyroidism, and my antibodies returned positive for Graves' disease.

Now, under specialized care, I have been on the road to recovery and to better understand my body and how interventions are affecting me.

This is my data and this is my journey.
